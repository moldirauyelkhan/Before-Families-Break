Before Families Break

AI, social workers and Kazakhstan's social orphans

In 2023, 21,387 children in Kazakhstan lived without parental care. Most of their parents are alive.

Can AI help child protection reach families before they break?

1. The problem

Why did these children lose their first family?

In the new documentary There Are Many of Us (Balgabayev Doc, 2026), ordinary Kazakhstani families talk about adopting and fostering: their fear of "bad genes," the opinions of relatives, the first months when a child tests every boundary. It is a film about finding children new families. This project asks the question that comes before it.

In 2023, Kazakhstan counted 21,387 orphans and children left without parental care (Bureau of National Statistics, cited in Mussabalinova, CABAR). Most are not orphans in the usual sense. They are "social orphans": children whose parents are alive. An earlier analysis found that 82.4% of children in institutions had living parents (CABAR, c. 2020). Nearly three quarters (73%) live under guardianship, mostly with relatives; 17% live in institutions; 8% are in foster families.

At first glance, the trend looks good. The number of children without parental care fell from about 24,200 in 2019 to 21,400 at the start of 2024 (Bizmedia), and the film cites 20,798 by official data. But the decline hides a harder picture. The number of children moving from institutions into families halved between 2015 and 2024, from 2,600 to 1,200. Some placements fail: in 2024, 235 children were returned to state institutions, 164 of them after guardians gave up (Inbusiness, citing statistics bureau data via Finprom). And every year the state takes parental rights from more than a thousand parents: more than 1,126 in 2025 (NewTimes.kz).

Work with struggling families is firefighting: authorities step in only once a situation is already critical.

Aigerim Mussabalinova, KIMEP University, describing a 2025 study (CABAR). The reasons families reach that point are ordinary: divorce, a parent's or child's illness, domestic violence. Yet the people who should catch these families early are very few.

The state does not even keep separate statistics on children removed from their families in emergencies. That admission came from Nasymzhan Ospanova, chair of the Committee for the Protection of Children's Rights (NewTimes.kz).

Who carries the cost? First, the children, especially those hardest to place: teenagers, children with disabilities, and siblings who must stay together. Second, families in crisis who get help only when it is too late to keep them together. Third, the social workers themselves, who have time only for emergencies.

Kazakhstan has built new laws and new family forms to find children new homes. But the system for keeping children in their first homes runs on very few people with very little time. Why does child protection act only after a family breaks, and who pays for that delay?

This is a question about institutions, not technology: about how a state spends its scarce attention.

2. Background and data

Counted only after the fact

Kazakhstan counts children who have lost families fairly well, but it barely counts the families who are about to lose them, which is exactly where prevention would work.

The headline numbers point the right way: fewer children without parental care, and most now live in families rather than institutions. But the flow out of institutions slowed, with placements into families halving from 2,600 in 2015 to 1,200 in 2024 (Inbusiness). And in 2023 the long fall in new cases reversed.

Where the data goes silent

Tap a blacked-out line to read what is missing.

Case file: what is not counted
  • No count of emergency removalsKazakhstan keeps no separate statistics on children removed from their families when their life or health is threatened. The most consequential decision in the system is the least measured.
  • No data on prevention work itselfThere are no public figures on how many struggling families are visited, how often, or for how long, nor on caseloads or paperwork time. Removal decisions are often based on police information rather than a social worker's assessment, because there are too few social workers (CABAR, c. 2020).
  • Totals that do not agreeThe same population appears as 21,387 (2023), 21,400 (start of 2024) and 20,798 (2025), and the 2023 breakdown leaves 332 children unaccounted for. Definitions and reporting dates shift between sources.
  • We know how many placements fail, not whyThe bureau publishes how many children return after a cancelled guardianship, patronage or adoption. Nothing is published on what support the family had, or who checked on them before it broke.
  • Who falls throughChildren whom relatives take in informally, and families who struggle but never reach the police, are invisible in every dataset.

How far can we trust the numbers? Counts of children already in the system are reasonably reliable, and the bureau publishes them by year. Almost nothing is measured about the months before a child enters it: no visits, no caseloads, no emergency removals. The data is strongest exactly where it is least useful for prevention.

4. The AI-assisted solution

The case-note assistant

A Kazakh–Russian AI scribe for guardianship specialists that cuts paperwork and, by rule, turns the saved hours into early visits to struggling families. The AI never assesses, scores or decides anything about a child.

One home visit, step by step

Who runs it. The Ministry of Education's guardianship bodies own it. The speech model is built on Kazakhstan's open speech corpora (ISSAI, Nazarbayev University), and all audio and text stay on servers in Kazakhstan.

What else it writes. The same assistant drafts routine emails, letters and referrals to other services, which Moldir proposed after asking why the help should stop at case notes. Every draft is checked and signed by the specialist. It never drafts court submissions, removal decisions or conclusions about a child, where a wrong word costs too much. Its savings are measured separately from the scribe's, so the evaluation can tell which part works.

If recording needs consent, won't abusive parents simply refuse?

An objection Moldir raised against her own project

Probably, and the assistant is not built to catch them. Refusing does not cancel the visit: the specialist dictates her own observations after leaving, a use the vendor's own guidance describes (Beam). Hours saved with families who agree can then be spent on any family, including those who refused. Spotting abuse is the specialist's job, not the software's; a tool designed to expose parents would be surveillance, and families would refuse even more.

The honest limit is that the assistant helps least, directly, in the most dangerous homes, which go through the child protection procedure rather than prevention visits. The risk the objection reveals is that refusing could start to look suspicious. Condition 1 forbids that, and the evaluation must remember that families who agree to recording may differ from those who refuse.

Who it reaches first. A one-year pilot in Karaganda region, compared with Kostanay region, which does not get the tool. The choice follows the data. In 2023 Karaganda had more children in institutions than any other region (461), and one of the highest rates for its child population. Kostanay has a similar rate and a similar Soviet-era institutional legacy, which makes it a fair comparison. Karaganda also has the most institutions of any region (8), and regions with more institutions may simply place more children in them. That is a further sign that the problem is the system's structure, not only families.

One limit: the pilot and comparison regions are both largely Russian-speaking, so a second phase should add a mostly Kazakh-speaking southern region to test condition 4 fully.

Where this already runs

All of these are English-language, mostly adult social care, and evaluated by councils or the vendor. Research behind several positive reports was commissioned by Beam itself, and experts still ask whether saved time goes to relationship-based work (Community Care). That is the honest limit of the evidence, and the reason to build independent evaluation into the Kazakh pilot.

Has an AI scribe ever actually helped a family?

An objection Moldir raised against her own project

Not that anyone has shown yet. Every case above measures hours saved and staff satisfaction, never what happened to the families. A systematic review found only eight empirical studies of AI-assisted case management worldwide (Li, Wang and Jian, 2025). The project rests on a chain of four steps, and only the first has been measured.

So the pilot must be judged on families, not on minutes saved. Measuring the second step, whether saved hours really turn into visits, is the first thing the evaluation has to do.

Can a note-taking tool help when a family's problem is poverty, addiction or a parent in prison?

An objection Moldir raised against her own project

Not by itself. The assistant only frees time, and time helps only where a family is struggling but could hold together with support. Some causes no visit can prevent: when a parent dies or goes to prison, the child still needs care, and the specialist's job becomes finding a good family home quickly. How Kazakhstan's cases split between these groups is not counted anywhere, which is one more gap in the data.

Worker time does matter. A review prepared for the Indiana legislature found a fair amount of evidence that lower caseloads for child-welfare workers are linked to better outcomes, including protection from abuse and more stable homes (Pew, 2019). But a visit only helps if the specialist has somewhere to send the family. That is why the verdict adds a sixth condition.

4. The AI-assisted solution: the bill

What it would cost, and who pays

Cost weight shown as ■
Data ethics■■■■■Recording families when the person holding the phone can remove their child.

The tool records families in their own homes, and the person holding the phone may later decide whether to remove their child. Under that imbalance, consent is a serious question, since recordings could become evidence against parents. Protection therefore has to be structural: recording only with a clear, refusable choice and no penalty for refusing, with the specialist dictating her own notes when a family says no; audio deleted once the note is signed; transcripts never used in court without a judge's order.

Whether families accept recording will depend less on the technology than on whether the process feels fair. Meers et al. (2026) find that public views of AI note-taking turn on privacy, feeling heard and transparency. Trials of AI scribes have also occasionally omitted information and recognised some accents less accurately, so every note must be checked by the specialist.

Labour■■■■□Saved time can become prevention, or a reason not to hire.

With 692 specialists for 6.9 million children, and 688 posts still missing, the risk is that the government counts the tool as staff. "70–190 specialists' worth of time" could easily be read as "we no longer need to hire." Hidden labour moves too: someone must check and correct every transcript, and native speakers must label Kazakh and code-switched speech to train the model, the invisible work behind every AI system.

Politics and governance■■■■□Who can open a family's file, and under which law.

Kazakhstan's Law on Personal Data (No. 94-V, 2013) has required personal data to be stored inside the country since 1 January 2016 (Chambers), which rules out a foreign cloud tool such as Magic Notes as it stands. The Law on Artificial Intelligence (No. 230-VIII), in force since 18 January 2026, sorts AI systems into risk levels and leaves the classification to the system's owner (EY). A tool that touches child-protection files should be classified as high-risk rather than left to self-assessment.

The sharpest risk is that a searchable archive of family conversations becomes attractive to police and other agencies. The answer is to make sure that archive never exists, and to lock what remains:

Don't keep the audio. Deleted automatically once the note is signed. What cannot be searched cannot be misused.
Keep only what the case needs. The note holds agreed actions and facts, not every word a parent said.
Purpose written into the rules. Notes are for supporting the family; any police or court use needs a judge's order.
Every opening is logged. An outside body, such as the Commissioner for Children's Rights, reviews the log and publishes how often records were opened and why.
Families see their own file. Parents can read and correct the note about them, so the record is made with them, not about them.

Each layer answers one of the three conditions for fairness that Meers et al. identify: privacy, feeling heard and transparency.

Economic■■■□□Not cheap, but small next to one child in an institution.

UK councils show the price range: Middlesbrough's one-year contract was about £33,000 (Find a Tender), North Northamptonshire's £48,000 (Find a Tender), and Bristol's £75,000 (Contracts Finder). Priced the same way across Kazakhstan's 20 regions and cities of republican significance, that would be roughly £0.7–1.5 million a year (our calculation), a ceiling rather than a budget. Because of data localisation the tool must be built locally, so the main costs are development, Kazakh speech-model training, servers inside the country and staff training. Who profits depends on who builds it.

Against that, prevention is valuable: a 2013 study put one child in an orphanage at about 2.1 million tenge a year, against about 137,000 tenge under guardianship (CABAR, c. 2020), roughly fifteen times cheaper. The tool does not need to be cheap to be worth it; it needs to keep a fairly small number of children out of institutions each year.

That argument has a danger in it. If savings become the goal, keeping children at home turns into a budget target, and a child could be left in an unsafe home to meet it. This is why the verdict rules out removal targets.

Environmental■□□□□Nearly two people's yearly electricity, on a coal grid.

Transcribing and summarising a visit is a small computing job. But Kazakhstan's electricity is still mostly coal: 62% of generation in 2025, from a coal fleet over 50 years old on average (Ember).

The verdict

Yes, for a pilot. Not yet for the country.

The case-note assistant is worth piloting because the shortage it addresses is real and measured in the state's own terms: 692 specialists for 6.9 million children, 688 short of the government's own standard. In the UK, AI scribes cut paperwork by 44–63%, and Kingston turned the savings into prevention services. But that evidence is self-reported, English-only and mostly from adult care, no study yet shows an AI scribe helping a family, and the tool records vulnerable families in their homes. So the answer is yes to a one-year pilot in Karaganda region, the region with the most children in institutions, and no to a national rollout until the pilot proves itself.

Does prevention push children to stay in homes that are bad for them?

An objection Moldir raised against her own project

This is the most serious risk, and there is research on it. A large US evaluation of family preservation programmes found that placement in foster care did not go down, while child safety was maintained (US HHS). An earlier review found that most randomised studies showed no significant fall in placements (Littell and Schuerman, 1995). Later meta-analyses are more positive but limited: a reduction for individual children, not for whole families (Bezeczky et al., 2020); an effect for families with many problems, but not where there was abuse or neglect (Al et al., 2012); and only a small effect on placement across 33 controlled studies (Lippens et al., 2025).

So the danger lies less in the tool than in the target. If "fewer orphans" becomes the goal, specialists could feel pushed to leave children where they are. The pilot closes that door:

  • The assistant never recommends keeping or removing a child. Specialists still remove children who are unsafe.
  • There are no removal targets. The pilot is never judged on fewer removals alone.
  • Earlier visits may find danger sooner, so a removal that happens earlier counts as a success, not a failure.
  • Child safety is measured alongside: repeat reports of abuse, and children returned from new families (condition 5).

The stop rule

The pilot ends, and the verdict becomes no, if saved time is absorbed into more paperwork or used to justify fewer posts, if refusals affect families' cases, if any recording is accessed outside the rules, if a target for fewer removals is set, or if repeat reports of abuse rise in Karaganda compared with Kostanay.

What the assistant cannot do

It cannot replace the 688 missing specialists. At best it gives the existing 692 back some of their week. It cannot bring back a parent who has died or gone to prison, and it cannot create services a district does not have. The real cure for firefighting is people; this tool only buys them time. That is why the verdict is a pilot, not a solution.
